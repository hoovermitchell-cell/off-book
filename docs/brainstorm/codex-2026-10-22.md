# Off Book brainstorm, 2026-10-22

Options for Mitch, not new decisions. My first choice is a review that finds where a student's prep ended and gives them one reason to return next club meeting.

Read: [AGENTS.md](../../AGENTS.md), [DESIGN.md](../../DESIGN.md), [decisions](../decisions.md), [research](../research/2026-10-21-research.md), and [backlog](../../backlog.md). `docs/review/staged-lines.md` was absent. Stages below mean decision 14's main line, alternatives, and real matches; their pass criteria remain open.

All v1 options keep student data in the browser and use reviewed text or deterministic templates. Stockfish evaluates moves; Maia predicts replies. Off-book play earns no line levels. ## 1. Ten reasons to come back weekly

1. Pin one existing prep position for the next club meeting, chosen after a drill or game review; leave it pinned until the student returns.
   Fit: The next visit has a concrete purpose, and missing a week erases nothing.

2. Revisit a previously missed prep move with its explanation hidden, then show the student's earlier attempt beside today's attempt.
   Fit: Improvement has a move and a dated record behind it; a failed retry simply stays in practice.

3. Keep a branch encounter log: which London or Caro-Kann replies appeared in entered games, and which the student has only drilled.
   Fit: Actual games leave marks in the binder without awarding experience for branches never played.

4. Let the student attach one question to a game position and reopen it at the next club meeting with a coach or partner.
   Fit: Returning resolves something the student cared about. Free-text questions are an added scope option; answers need not be generated.

5. Offer a weekly rehearsal against one Maia-predicted reply from the student's current stage, with its existing lesson first if unfamiliar.
   Fit: The record shows what was practiced; the reply stays available after the week ends. Stage selection rules still need Mitch's decision.

6. Pair students at a physical board to play a known prep branch, then swap sides and compare their recollection with the app.
   Fit: A clubmate supplies the reason to return. Log this as partner practice, separate from app-tested moves and real-match evidence; added scope.

7. Where the repertoire contains two move orders reaching the same position, alternate them on later visits and ask for the next prep move.
   Fit: The student earns evidence of recognizing the position rather than memorizing one sequence. Use only verified transpositions.

8. Put a dated, replayable drill attempt beside each line's stage, so a student can return to improve the evidence behind that stage.
   Fit: The record shows moves, hints and corrections. It doesn't invent a promotion rule for the still-open meaning of "without fail."

9. Pair an opening-filtered Lichess puzzle with a relevant existing lesson, then offer another from that opening on a later visit.
   Fit: Solved positions accumulate as work done. No standalone puzzle mode, own-game puzzles, or assumed puzzle credit toward line levels.

10. Let students choose a modest weekly goal, such as reviewing one game or revisiting two prep positions, and retain completed work in a dated log.
    Fit: Students can change the next goal or skip a week without losing progress; no overdue count or broken streak.

## 2. Five ways to review a hand-entered game

Shared entry rule: choose the student's color, enter moves on the board, then replay and confirm the record. Accept an unfinished scoresheet up to its last reliable move; don't guess the rest. A position entered without its preceding moves can receive position analysis but cannot prove who left prep first. Store the repertoire version and known stage at play time; if unknown, label comparison against today's prep. Evaluate from the student's perspective, showing search limits and numbers beside verdicts.

1. **Find the prep boundary.** Replay the confirmed moves against the student's staged repertoire, recognizing equivalent legal positions. Mark who first left the selected prep, distinguish an unlearned branch from a missed learned move, and flag any later return to prep. Stockfish checks whether the departure actually lost value; Maia supplies predicted replies, not proof of a mistake.
   End with the last familiar position and one existing lesson to revisit. Going off book alone never earns a mistake glyph.

2. **Review one costly moment.** Among confirmed student moves, offer the largest Stockfish loss that clears the chosen mistake threshold, with before/after evaluations and a short engine continuation. Say when none clears it; keep forced-mate scores distinct from pawn scores.
   Show a template explanation only when its detector supports it. Link to a staged lesson only when relevant, and distinguish an opening error from a later error.

3. **Start with the student's question.** The student selects a move they worried about and enters a candidate alternative on an analysis board. Compare both with Stockfish at the same search budget, then inspect Maia's predicted responses at the selected rating.
   Show whether the position was in their staged prep. This is unscored game analysis, with no saved own-game puzzle or automated claim about the student's thought process.

4. **Compare two real encounters.** Match a confirmed position in this game to an earlier entered game, then display the student's choices, prep status and Stockfish evaluations side by side. If no matching position exists, say so.
   A different result doesn't prove better opening play. Show the actual decisions, using the repertoire version associated with each game where available.

5. **Replay the departure against Maia.** After review, optionally start a practice continuation from the confirmed prep boundary, reveal the relevant existing lesson, and let the student play through to mate, resignation or draw.
   Keep that continuation separate from the historical game. Stockfish flags mistakes; Maia plays replies. This adds a sparring entry point, not an own-game puzzle, and off-book moves cannot level the line.

Where a separate coach device is useful, propose a student-selected summary file transferred directly by removable storage and opened locally, with no upload, email or cloud folder. This is an alternative transport proposal; the decided later sync-code mechanism remains unshaped. ## 4. Three risks I didn't find named in the documents read

These are hypotheses, not observed failures or a claim about every prior conversation.

1. **A legal transcription error could receive a convincing chess explanation.** Move legality won't catch every wrong square, and a template could criticize a move the student never played.
   Cheap test: Give a few students an authentic scoresheet and a prefilled entry containing one wrong but legal move, with a legal continuation. Ask them to check the entry against the sheet before viewing analysis. If they miss the error, test position checkpoints during entry.

2. **The real-match stage could measure opportunity as much as skill.** A student who rarely gets the right color or opening may have little chance to demonstrate a rehearsed branch, while another gets it repeatedly.
   Cheap test: Hand-label a small set of existing club games for opportunities to enter London or Caro-Kann prep. Compare each student's opportunities with their execution when the position actually occurred. If evidence is sparse, show "not yet encountered" and take promotion rules back to Mitch.

3. **Shared Chromebooks could mix two students' prep histories.** Device-only storage leaves ownership ambiguous if students use the same browser profile; no server is needed for the wrong student to inherit a stage.
   Cheap test: On a school device, have two volunteers take turns using their normal sign-in procedure and inspect which progress survives. If they share state, shape an explicit local-profile or backup/restore workflow before counting individual progress.

## 5. My top three

1. **Prep-boundary review (review 1).** It completes the product's central promise: show exactly where learned prep met a real opponent. Start with confirmed opening moves; a complete scoresheet shouldn't be required to get useful feedback.
2. **One pinned position for next club (idea 1).** It turns that review into a reason to return, using an existing lesson and a small local record. It also gives students without a new game somewhere concrete to resume.
3. **Prep evidence on the student's screen (C1, later).** A coach can inspect the moves behind a stage without a roster, transfer mechanism or aggregate dashboard. Test it as a supervised walkthrough before investing in the later coach view.
